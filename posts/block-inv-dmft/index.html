Deriving the DMFT cavity equation using the matrix block inverse formula

8 Nov 2026 15 min

In many high-dimensional linear problems, I want to calculate how one variable (or coordinate) responds to constraints on the remaining. A classic example is the disordered linear dynamical system, where we have a large system of sites, arranged into a vector hhh, which undergo random but coupled dynamics. The goal is to find an equation to describe the dynamics of a single site, marginalizing out the effects of the other sites. This is a rich system; Blake gives a nice introduction in his 2026 review paper with Cengiz Pehlevan, and Mark wrote up a detailed walkthrough of it on his blog.

Two other problems with similar structure:

  1. High-dimensional linear regression. We have a random function "vector" y(x)y(x)y(x) whose expected covariance is known. We want to find an equation that predicts the distribution of a single element y(x0)y(x_0)y(x0​) conditioned on observations {y(xi)}i\{y(x_i)\}_i{y(xi​)}i​ at several other training points.
  2. Gaussian free field. We have a free field ϕ(x)\phi(x)ϕ(x) with kinetic operator L=−∇2+m2L = -\nabla^2 + m^2L=−∇2+m2. We place a point source at the endpoint x0x_0x0​ of a semi-infinite 1D lattice. What's ϕ(x0)\phi(x_0)ϕ(x0​)?

What all of these problems have in common is that they're linear problems that can be solved by the matrix block inversion formula. I thought that this formula was pretty opaque, and I wanted to better understand where it comes from. The point of this blog post is to give a few different derivations of it, and then show how to use it to solve the disordered linear dynamical system. I'll leave the other two problems as exercises :—)

Why is this about block inversion?

At the core of all these problems is the linear relation j=Mhj = Mhj=Mh, where the symmetric matrix MMM is known, but jjj and hhh are both only partially-known to us, with some unknown components we need to solve for. For example, the linear dynamical system is governed by h˙=−Ah+j\dot h = -Ah + jh˙=−Ah+j, which can be written as j=(A+ddt)hj = \left(A + \frac{d}{dt}\right)hj=(A+dtd​)h. We want to isolate the behavior of a single site (wlog, index 0), which we can depict as separated from the remaining "bath" variables:

[j0jb]=[A00+ddtAb0⊤Ab0Abb+ddtI][h0hb]=:[s    c⊤    c    B    ][h0hb].\begin{align*} \left[\begin{array}{c} j_0 \\[0.4em] \hdashline \rule[-2em]{0pt}{4.5em} j_b \end{array}\right] &= \left[\begin{array}{c:c} A_{00} + \frac{d}{dt} & \qquad A_{b0}^\top \qquad \\[0.4em] \hdashline \rule[-2em]{0pt}{4.5em} A_{b0} & \qquad A_{bb} + \frac{d}{dt} I \qquad \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{c} h_0 \\[0.4em] \hdashline \rule[-2em]{0pt}{4.5em} h_b \end{array}\right] \\[4.5em] &=: \left[\begin{array}{c:c} s & \qquad\;\; c^\top \qquad\;\; \\[0.4em] \hdashline \rule[-2em]{0pt}{4.5em} c & \qquad\;\; B \qquad\;\; \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{c} h_0 \\[0.4em] \hdashline \rule[-2em]{0pt}{4.5em} h_b \end{array}\right] . \end{align*}​j0​jb​​​​​=​A00​+dtd​Ab0​​Ab0⊤​Abb​+dtd​I​​​​h0​hb​​​​=:​sc​c⊤B​​​​h0​hb​​​​.​

This site-bath structure makes the problem interesting — in some sense it precludes naive spectral approaches, since the site basis is privileged. In the second line I've renamed the blocks for simplicity of notation: sss (for "site") is a scalar, ccc (for "coupling") is a vector, and BBB (for "bath") is a matrix.

To solve for h0(t)h_0(t)h0​(t) without explicit reference to hb(t)h_b(t)hb​(t), we clearly need to invert M:=A+ddtM:=A + \frac{d}{dt}M:=A+dtd​ in terms of the blocks sss, ccc, and BBB. The formula is pretty complicated:

[h0hb]=[1s−c⊤B−1c−c⊤B−1s−c⊤B−1c−B−1cs−c⊤B−1cB−1+B−1c c⊤B−1s−c⊤B−1c][j0jb].\left[\begin{array}{c} \rule[-1.3em]{0pt}{2.9em} h_0 \\[0.4em] \hdashline \rule[-2em]{0pt}{4.5em} h_b \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{c:c} \rule[-1.3em]{0pt}{2.9em} \dfrac{1}{s - c^\top B^{-1} c} & \quad -\dfrac{c^\top B^{-1}}{s - c^\top B^{-1} c} \quad \\[0.4em] \hdashline \rule[-2em]{0pt}{4.5em} -\dfrac{B^{-1} c}{s - c^\top B^{-1} c} & \quad B^{-1} + \dfrac{B^{-1} c\, c^\top B^{-1}}{s - c^\top B^{-1} c} \quad \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{c} \rule[-1.3em]{0pt}{2.9em} j_0 \\[0.4em] \hdashline \rule[-2em]{0pt}{4.5em} j_b \end{array}\right] .​h0​hb​​​​=​s−c⊤B−1c1​−s−c⊤B−1cB−1c​​−s−c⊤B−1cc⊤B−1​B−1+s−c⊤B−1cB−1cc⊤B−1​​​​​j0​jb​​​​.

I think it's important to understand what's going on in this formula. I want to give a few different derivations, all of which give some physical intuition for this formula, and for the linear dynamical system. If you don't care about that, you can skip to the next section.

Using Gaussian elimination

This is the most straightforward method, but imo the least illuminating. We just write the two block rows of j=Mhj = Mhj=Mh:

j0=s h0+c⊤hbjb=c h0+B hb.\begin{aligned} j_0 &= s\, h_0 + c^\top h_b \\ j_b &= c\, h_0 + B\, h_b . \end{aligned}j0​jb​​=sh0​+c⊤hb​=ch0​+Bhb​.​

Solve for the bath site variables using the bath row equation: hb=B−1(jb−c h0)h_b = B^{-1}\left(j_b - c\, h_0\right)hb​=B−1(jb​−ch0​). Substitute this into the site row and collect h0h_0h0​:

(s−c⊤B−1c)h0=j0−c⊤B−1jb.\left(s - c^\top B^{-1} c\right) h_0 = j_0 - c^\top B^{-1} j_b .(s−c⊤B−1c)h0​=j0​−c⊤B−1jb​.

The quantity (s−c⊤B−1c)\left(s - c^\top B^{-1} c\right)(s−c⊤B−1c) is called the Schur complement. Dividing it from both sides gives the top row of the block inverse, and plugging h0h_0h0​ back into our equation for hbh_bhb​ gives the bottom row.

As we'll soon see, the Schur complement shows up a lot in these problems. Sadly, this derivation didn't give me much intuition for what it means, or why it has that functional form. Also, the Schur complement is dimensionful; we know from physics that oftentimes the key structural insight comes from trying to extract dimension-free quantities. The next method (using the Sherman-Morrison formula) does this directly.

Using the Sherman-Morrison formula

Notice that the site-bath coupling is the annoying part of the inverse. If we could just nix it, c=0c=0c=0, then we'd have

[s00B]−1=[1/s00B−1]\begin{bmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & B \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/s & 0 \\ 0 & B^{-1} \end{bmatrix}[s0​0B​]−1=[1/s0​0B−1​]

since that's how block-diagonal matrices work. This is intuitive — the site and the bath don't talk, so their inverses are independent. Can we just sneak the coupling back in somehow? A useful starting observation is that the uncoupled interaction matrix is a low-rank perturbation of the full interaction matrix:

M=[s00B]+e p⊤+p e⊤M = \begin{bmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & B \end{bmatrix} + e\,p^\top + p\, e^\topM=[s0​0B​]+ep⊤+pe⊤

where p⊤=[ 0    c⊤ ]p^\top = \left[\,0 \;\; c^\top\,\right]p⊤=[0c⊤] contains the couplings, and e=[1,0,…,0]⊤e = \left[1, 0, \dots, 0\right]^\tope=[1,0,…,0]⊤ is the first canonical basis vector. The problem has now transformed: we want to invert a low-rank perturbation of a matrix whose inverse we already know. This is where the Sherman-Morrison formula comes in. First we factor out the uncoupled part:

M−1=(I+e q⊤+p~ e⊤)−1[s00B]−1M^{-1} = \left( I + e\,q^\top + \tilde p\, e^\top\right)^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & B \end{bmatrix}^{-1}M−1=(I+eq⊤+p~​e⊤)−1[s0​0B​]−1

where p~⊤:=[ 0    (B−1c)⊤ ]\tilde p^\top := \left[\,0 \;\; \left(B^{-1}c\right)^\top\,\right]p~​⊤:=[0(B−1c)⊤] contains the coupling as seen through the uncoupled inverse, and q:=p/sq := p/sq:=p/s. I'll leave it as an exercise to go through the same steps as in my previous blog post; as a hint, it's useful to observe that e⊤e=1e^\top e=1e⊤e=1 and e⊤q=e⊤p~=0e^\top q = e^\top \tilde p = 0e⊤q=e⊤p~​=0. M−1=(I−11−g(e q⊤+p~ e⊤−p~ q⊤−g e e⊤))[s00B]−1=(I−11−g[−gc⊤/sB−1c−B−1c c⊤/s])[s00B]−1=[1s(1−g)−c⊤B−1s(1−g)−B−1cs(1−g)B−1+B−1c c⊤B−1s(1−g)].\begin{aligned} M^{-1} &= \left( I - \frac{1}{1-g} \left( e\,q^\top + \tilde p\, e^\top - \tilde p\,q^\top - g\, e\, e^\top \right) \right) \begin{bmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & B \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \\[1.5em] &= \left( I - \frac{1}{1-g} \begin{bmatrix} -g & c^\top / s \\ B^{-1}c & -B^{-1}c\,c^\top / s \end{bmatrix} \right) \begin{bmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & B \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \\[1.5em] &= \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{1}{s\left(1-g\right)} & \qquad -\dfrac{c^\top B^{-1}}{s\left(1-g\right)} \\[2em] -\dfrac{B^{-1}c}{s\left(1-g\right)} & \qquad B^{-1} + \dfrac{B^{-1}c\,c^\top B^{-1}}{s\left(1-g\right)} \end{bmatrix} . \end{aligned}M−1​=(I−1−g1​(eq⊤+p~​e⊤−p~​q⊤−gee⊤))[s0​0B​]−1=(I−1−g1​[−gB−1c​c⊤/s−B−1cc⊤/s​])[s0​0B​]−1=​s(1−g)1​−s(1−g)B−1c​​−s(1−g)c⊤B−1​B−1+s(1−g)B−1cc⊤B−1​​​.​

Notice that our quantity g=q⊤p~g=q^\top \tilde pg=q⊤p~​ has two nice properties: a) it shows up everywhere, and b) it's dimensionless! I think this is a strong signal that it's worth trying to interpret its meaning. We can get some intuition by relying on the visual derivation in my previous blog post. First, our quantity p~\tilde pp~​ is exactly analogous to the p~\tilde pp~​ from the blog post. It represents the coupling that remains after we factor out the uncoupled inverse. Then, since p~e⊤\tilde p e^\topp~​e⊤ skews the bath variables according to the first site's activity, and eq⊤e q^\topeq⊤ skews the site variable according to the bath's activity, the quantity g=q⊤p~g=q^\top \tilde pg=q⊤p~​ is akin to a gain, or echo: it measures the strength of the round-trip effect of the site on itself through the bath.

This interpretation of ggg becomes a lot more explicit when we solve the inverse problem using an iterative method. That's what we'll do next.

Using Gauss-Seidel iteration (alternating minimization)

In non-convex optimization, alternating minimization is a powerful method. (An example from neuroscience is sparse coding w/ dictionary learning.) The idea is that even though the full problem is jointly non-convex, the sub-problems where we optimize only one variable while keeping the others fixed are often individually convex. So, we just alternate between minimizing each variable separately. Applying that idea to blockwise inversion is called Gauss-Seidel iteration: we solve the site row for h0h_0h0​ holding the bath fixed, then solve the bath row for hbh_bhb​ holding the site fixed, and repeat.

h0(k+1)=1s(j0−c⊤hb(k))hb(k+1)=B−1(jb−c h0(k+1)).\begin{aligned} h_0^{(k+1)} &= \frac{1}{s}\left(j_0 - c^\top h_b^{(k)}\right) \\[1em] h_b^{(k+1)} &= B^{-1}\left(j_b - c\, h_0^{(k+1)}\right) . \end{aligned}h0(k+1)​hb(k+1)​​=s1​(j0​−c⊤hb(k)​)=B−1(jb​−ch0(k+1)​).​

The uncoupled bath satisfies hb(0)=B−1jbh_b^{(0)} = B^{-1} j_bhb(0)​=B−1jb​. How does the site react? It responds to both its drive j0j_0j0​ and the bath: h0(1)=1s(j0−c⊤B−1jb)h_0^{(1)} = \frac{1}{s}\left(j_0 - c^\top B^{-1} j_b\right)h0(1)​=s1​(j0​−c⊤B−1jb​). Then the bath reacts to this, hb(1)=B−1jb−B−1c h0(1)h_b^{(1)} = B^{-1} j_b - B^{-1} c\, h_0^{(1)}hb(1)​=B−1jb​−B−1ch0(1)​, and that reaction comes back to the site as h0(2)=(1+g)h0(1)h_0^{(2)} = \left(1 + g\right) h_0^{(1)}h0(2)​=(1+g)h0(1)​, where g=1sc⊤B−1cg=\frac{1}{s}c^\top B^{-1} cg=s1​c⊤B−1c is the same dimensionless gain from the previous section. Each round trip through the bath multiplies the latest correction by ggg, so

h0(k)=(1+g+g2+⋯+gk−1)h0(1)  ⟶  h0(1)1−g=j0−c⊤B−1jbs(1−g),h_0^{(k)} = \left(1 + g + g^2 + \dots + g^{k-1}\right) h_0^{(1)} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{h_0^{(1)}}{1-g} = \frac{j_0 - c^\top B^{-1} j_b}{s\left(1-g\right)} ,h0(k)​=(1+g+g2+⋯+gk−1)h0(1)​⟶1−gh0(1)​​=s(1−g)j0​−c⊤B−1jb​​,

which is the top row of the block inverse again. Therefore 1/(1−g)=1+g+g2+g3+…1/\left(1-g\right)= 1 + g + g^2 + g^3 + \dots1/(1−g)=1+g+g2+g3+… is the cumulative effect of all the echoes, cycling through the site and the bath and back to the site again, multiple times.Technically the iteration only converges when ∣g∣<1\lvert g \rvert < 1∣g∣<1, but our solution is correct as long as g≠1g \neq 1g=1. In the case ∣g∣>1\lvert g \rvert > 1∣g∣>1, iteration is simply not the right approach.

Returning to the linear dynamical system

Our problem is j(t)=(A+ddt)h(t)j(t) = \left(A + \frac{d}{dt}\right)h(t)j(t)=(A+dtd​)h(t), where h(t)h(t)h(t) contains the site activities, j(t)j(t)j(t) contains the per-site external drive, and AAA is the time-independent interaction matrix. The trouble here is the d/dtd/dtd/dt, which is a bit hard to handle. Things will get a lot easier if we Fourier transform, since this takes d/dtd/dtd/dt to iωi\omegaiω, i.e., just a scalar times the identity matrix:

[ȷ~0ȷ~b]=[A00+iωAb0⊤Ab0Abb+iωI][h~0h~b]=:[sc⊤cB][h~0h~b].\begin{align*} \begin{bmatrix} \tilde\jmath_0 \\ \tilde\jmath_b \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} A_{00} + i\omega & A_{b0}^\top \\ A_{b0} & A_{bb} + i\omega I \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \tilde h_0 \\ \tilde h_b \end{bmatrix} \\[1.5em] &=: \begin{bmatrix} s & c^\top \\ c & B \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \tilde h_0 \\ \tilde h_b \end{bmatrix} . \end{align*}[~​0​~​b​​]​=[A00​+iωAb0​​Ab0⊤​Abb​+iωI​][h~0​h~b​​]=:[sc​c⊤B​][h~0​h~b​​].​

Here I'm using tildes to denote Fourier-space variables, i.e., functions of ω\omegaω. We want to solve for the site activity h0(t)h_0(t)h0​(t) in terms of j(t)j(t)j(t). To do this we can just invert (using the block inversion formula) and read off the top row:

h~0=1A00+iω−r~(ȷ~0−Ab0⊤(Abb+iωI)−1 ȷ~b)\tilde h_0 = \frac{1}{A_{00} + i\omega - \tilde r} \left( \tilde\jmath_0 - A_{b0}^\top \left(A_{bb}+i\omega I\right)^{-1}\, \tilde\jmath_b \right)h~0​=A00​+iω−r~1​(~​0​−Ab0⊤​(Abb​+iωI)−1~​b​)

where I've introduced r~:=Ab0⊤(Abb+iωI)−1Ab0=c⊤B−1c\tilde r := A_{b0}^\top \left(A_{bb}+i\omega I\right)^{-1} A_{b0}=c^\top B^{-1} cr~:=Ab0⊤​(Abb​+iωI)−1Ab0​=c⊤B−1c. Our equation for h~0\tilde h_0h~0​ is, in some sense, complete: it does not directly depend on the other site trajectories. But, the annoying thing about our equation is that it still depends on the drive's bath variables, as well as all the bath couplings. If the system has permutation symmetry — there are no privileged sites — then we should expect to be able to simplify this further, to somehow "average out" the aggregate effect of the bath. This requires studying the remaining bath terms.

The first remaining bath term is r~\tilde rr~. A few notes:

  1. Since r~=sg\tilde r= sgr~=sg, where g=1sc⊤B−1cg=\frac{1}{s}c^\top B^{-1} cg=s1​c⊤B−1c is the same dimensionless gain from the previous section, you can think of it as a dimensionful gain factor.
  2. If AAA is a symmetric random matrix with i.i.d. entries (zero mean and variance 1/N1/N1/N for NNN sites), then ccc is independent of B−1B^{-1}B−1, and in the large-bath limit the random quadratic form r~=c⊤B−1c\tilde r = c^\top B^{-1} cr~=c⊤B−1c concentrates to its expectation: r~=c⊤B−1c→Ec[c⊤B−1c]=tr[B−1],\tilde r = c^\top B^{-1} c \to \mathbb{E}_c\mleft[c^\top B^{-1} c\mright] = \mathrm{tr}\mleft[B^{-1}\mright],r~=c⊤B−1c→Ec​[c⊤B−1c]=tr[B−1], where tr\mathrm{tr}tr is the normalized trace (i.e., trace divided by matrix dimension). This "deterministic equivalent" is therefore a consequence of self-averaging in the large bath limit.
  3. If the system is large enough that A00≪tr[B−1]A_{00} \ll \mathrm{tr}\mleft[B^{-1}\mright]A00​≪tr[B−1], then we can discard that particular coupling. Then the site 0 inverse matrix element converges to a deterministic quantity: 1A00+iω−r~→1iω−r~.\frac{1}{A_{00}+i\omega-\tilde r} \to \frac{1}{i\omega-\tilde r}.A00​+iω−r~1​→iω−r~1​. If the system also has permutation symmetry, then the full system acts the same as the bath by itself, and all the diagonal elements are equal to the normalized trace (which, recall, is just r~\tilde rr~): 1iω−r~=tr[(A+iωI)−1]=tr[B−1]=r~.\frac{1}{i\omega-\tilde r} = \mathrm{tr}\mleft[(A+i\omega I)^{-1}\mright] = \mathrm{tr}\mleft[B^{-1}\mright] = \tilde r.iω−r~1​=tr[(A+iωI)−1]=tr[B−1]=r~. This leave-one-out approximation gives a self-consistent equation for r~\tilde rr~, which we can just solve! This is what I meant earlier by "averaging out the aggregate effect of the bath." Under the (pretty reasonable) assumptions we made, we don't actually need to know any of the bath details to solve how the bath actually interacts with the site (through r~\tilde rr~).
  4. Finally, a note on nomenclature: in this particular problem, r~\tilde rr~ is often called the memory kernel (it'll become clear why later).

The second remaining bath term is u~:=−Ab0⊤(Abb+iωI)−1 ȷ~b=c⊤B−1ȷ~b\tilde u := -A_{b0}^\top \left(A_{bb}+i\omega I\right)^{-1}\, \tilde\jmath_b=c^\top B^{-1} \tilde\jmath_bu~:=−Ab0⊤​(Abb​+iωI)−1~​b​=c⊤B−1~​b​. A few more notes:

  1. Consider the bath activities if we nixed the coupling, c=0c=0c=0, or equivalently just removed site zero entirely. I'll use hb∖0h_{b\setminus 0}hb∖0​ to denote these counterfactual bath activities. In this scenario, we'd have B−1ȷ~b=h~b∖0B^{-1} \tilde\jmath_b = \tilde h_{b\setminus 0}B−1~​b​=h~b∖0​. Then u~=−c⊤h~b∖0\tilde u = -c^\top \tilde h_{b\setminus 0}u~=−c⊤h~b∖0​.
  2. If we assume again that AAA has i.i.d. entries, then ccc is independent of hb∖0h_{b\setminus 0}hb∖0​, and in the large-bath limit u~\tilde uu~ converges to a Gaussian process (by central limit theorem). The autocovariance of the GP is just the autocovariance of h~b∖0\tilde h_{b\setminus 0}h~b∖0​.
  3. If we assume self-consistency again (i.e., that the permutation symmetry + large bath implies that the full system acts the same as the bath by itself) then the autocovariance of h~b∖0\tilde h_{b\setminus 0}h~b∖0​ is equal to the autocovariance of h~\tilde hh~ which is equal to the autocovariance of h~0\tilde h_0h~0​.
  4. Finally, a note on nomenclature: in this particular problem, u~\tilde uu~ is often called the noise term since it's a Gaussian process that does not directly respond to either h0h_0h0​ or j0j_0j0​.

We've now killed all the bath variables (yay!). After rearranging, here's what's left:

iω h~0=ȷ~0+u~+r~ h~0.i\omega\, \tilde h_0 = \tilde\jmath_0 + \tilde u + \tilde r\, \tilde h_0 .iωh~0​=~​0​+u~+r~h~0​.

Now we finally Fourier transform back, using the convolution theorem for the product r~ h~0\tilde r \,\tilde h_0r~h~0​:

ddth0(t)=j0(t)+u(t)+∫−∞tdt′ r(t−t′) h0(t′).\frac{d}{dt} h_0(t) = j_0(t) + u(t) + \int_{-\infty}^{t} dt'\, r(t-t')\, h_0(t') .dtd​h0​(t)=j0​(t)+u(t)+∫−∞t​dt′r(t−t′)h0​(t′).

This is quite amazing: we have an ODE for a single site's dynamics that explicitly depends only on the site's own history (through the memory kernel rrr) and its own external drive j0j_0j0​. The dependence on the bath is implicit; it lives in the autocovariance of the Gaussian process uuu, and in the functional form of rrr. We already obtained a self-consistent equation for rrr earlier; I'll leave it to you to do the same for uuu. (You can find details in Mark's blogpost and Blake's paper.)

To recap, the main cavity idea here is exact: it follows from the block-inverse formula, which is straight up correct. Then, to simplify even more, we make the following extra approximations:

  • concentration: with large bath, things self-average and many random variables converge to deterministic limits
  • cavity (leave-one-out) approximation: when we remove the site, nothing much changes in the bath (at least, as far as its correlations are concerned). This gives self-consistency: noise autocovariance and memory kernel are shared across all sites. This allows us to solve for them with no dependence on the bath's details.

Two exercises

  1. Kernel regression. We have a random function y(x)y(x)y(x) with zero mean and known covariance K(x,x′)K(x, x')K(x,x′), so its values at a test point and at a set of training points are jointly Gaussian:
