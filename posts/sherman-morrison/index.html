A visual derivation of the Sherman-Morrison formula

6 Oct 2026 8 min

Here's a somewhat surprising fact about matrix inversion:

The inverse of a rank-one perturbation of AAA
equals a rank-one perturbation of A−1A^{-1}A−1

…assuming all the inverses exist, yada yada. This fact is captured by the Sherman-Morrison formula:

(A+p q⊤)−1=A−1−A−1p q⊤A−11+q⊤A−1p.\left(A+p\, q^\top\right)^{-1} = A^{-1} - \frac{A^{-1}p\, q^\top A^{-1}}{1 + q^\top A^{-1} p}.(A+pq⊤)−1=A−1−1+q⊤A−1pA−1pq⊤A−1​.

I thought this formula was super opaque and unintuitive. So, on a plane ride back from NYC, I re-derived it using a visual argument. It turns out this argument is on Wikipedia so sadly my derivation isn't that cool. But I wanna show it to you anyways! Plus, I have a widget, so that's extra points for me.

A visual derivation of the Sherman-Morrison formula

We want to invert M:=A+p q⊤M:=A + p\, q^\topM:=A+pq⊤. Let's factor it out: first, we invert AAA, then we composite it with a correction that comes from the perturbation:

(A+p q⊤)−1=(I+A−1p q⊤)−1A−1=:(I+p~ q⊤)−1A−1\left(A + p\, q^\top\right)^{-1} = \left(I + A^{-1}p\, q^\top\right)^{-1} A^{-1} =: \left(I + \tilde p\, q^\top\right)^{-1} A^{-1}(A+pq⊤)−1=(I+A−1pq⊤)−1A−1=:(I+p~​q⊤)−1A−1

where I defined p~=A−1p\tilde p = A^{-1}pp~​=A−1p. The multiplicative correction from the perturbation is the inverse of a perturbation of the identity. This is something we can understand geometrically. The transformation v′=(I+p~ q⊤)vv' = \left(I + \tilde p\, q^\top\right)vv′=(I+p~​q⊤)v basically says: "Return the original vector vvv, but skewed along direction p~\tilde pp~​, and the amount of skew is equal to the overlap of qqq and vvv." Here's an illustration:

Clearly, (I+p~ q⊤)−1\left(I + \tilde p\, q^\top\right)^{-1}(I+p~​q⊤)−1 is the "un-skew" operation: from v′v'v′ we should subtract (q⊤v)p~\left(q^\top v\right)\tilde p(q⊤v)p~​. Of course, we don't know vvv, so we don't immediately know the skew strength q⊤vq^\top vq⊤v. But here's a trick:

q⊤v′=q⊤(I+p~ q⊤)v=(q⊤v)(1+q⊤p~)  ⟹  q⊤v=q⊤v′(1+q⊤p~).\begin{align*} q^\top v' &= q^\top \left(I + \tilde p\, q^\top\right) v = \left(q^\top v\right)\left(1 + q^\top \tilde p\right)\\ \implies q^\top v &= \frac{q^\top v'}{\left(1 + q^\top \tilde p\right)}. \end{align*}q⊤v′⟹q⊤v​=q⊤(I+p~​q⊤)v=(q⊤v)(1+q⊤p~​)=(1+q⊤p~​)q⊤v′​.​

This equation tells me that, after skewing along p~\tilde pp~​, we might've also moved along the direction qqq, depending on how much p~\tilde pp~​ and qqq are aligned. This means the overlap between qqq and the image v′v'v′ misestimates the original skew strength by that alignment factor. We just need to correct for that misestimation by dividing the factor out. Rewriting this same argument mathematically, we get

(I+p~ q⊤)−1v′=v′−p~(q⊤v)=v′−p~ q⊤v′1+q⊤p~=(I−p~ q⊤1+q⊤p~)v′.\begin{aligned} \left(I + \tilde p\, q^\top\right)^{-1} v' &= v' - \tilde p\left(q^\top v\right) = v' - \frac{\tilde p\, q^\top v'}{1 + q^\top \tilde p} \\ &= \left(I - \frac{\tilde p\, q^\top}{1 + q^\top \tilde p}\right) v' . \end{aligned}(I+p~​q⊤)−1v′​=v′−p~​(q⊤v)=v′−1+q⊤p~​p~​q⊤v′​=(I−1+q⊤p~​p~​q⊤​)v′.​

That's it! We solved for the inverse using a simple geometric argument. Putting it all together:

(A+p q⊤)−1=(I+A−1p q⊤)−1A−1=(I−A−1p q⊤1+q⊤A−1p)A−1=A−1−A−1p q⊤A−11+q⊤A−1p.\begin{aligned} \left(A + p\, q^\top\right)^{-1} &= \left(I + A^{-1}p\, q^\top\right)^{-1} A^{-1} \\ &= \left(I - \frac{A^{-1}p\, q^\top}{1 + q^\top A^{-1} p}\right) A^{-1} \\ &= A^{-1} - \frac{A^{-1}p\, q^\top A^{-1}}{1 + q^\top A^{-1} p} . \end{aligned}(A+pq⊤)−1​=(I+A−1pq⊤)−1A−1=(I−1+q⊤A−1pA−1pq⊤​)A−1=A−1−1+q⊤A−1pA−1pq⊤A−1​.​

We derived the Sherman-Morrison formula. My interpretation is that the perturbed inverse is basically just the original inverse, followed by a rank-one correction along the skew direction. The normalizing term q⊤A−1pq^\top A^{-1} pq⊤A−1p in the denominator ensures that we don't misestimate the effect of the perturbation.

The bias term of generalization in linear regression

We can use the Sherman-Morrison formula to derive the expected bias of linear regression in the heavily overparameterized (limited data) regime. Here's how.

The data matrix XXX is short and stout: X∈Rn×dX\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}X∈Rn×d, with n≪dn \ll dn≪d. Its rows, which index the nnn data samples, are quite outnumbered by its columns, which index the data dimension ddd. We should think of XXX as a random variable: we've drawn some particular samples from a Gaussian distribution with expected covariance Λ\LambdaΛ. Let's work in the PCA basis of the input distribution, so Λ\LambdaΛ is diagonal and full-rank.This is quite different from the sample covariance X⊤XX^\top XX⊤X, which is singular due to the overparameterization.

The labels are scalar, noiseless, linear: y=w⋆⊤xy = w_\star^\top xy=w⋆⊤​x. You can add noise later if you want, but here I'll keep things simple. The unregularized min-norm estimator is well-known: w^(X)=Pw⋆\hat w(X) = P w_\starw^(X)=Pw⋆​, where P∈Rd×dP \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}P∈Rd×d is the projector onto the (strict) subspace spanned by the data samples, and is therefore also a random variable. This spanning subspace has dimension nnn (assuming the nnn data samples are linearly independent, which they should be, unless you got super unlucky somehow). Therefore Tr[P]=n\mathrm{Tr}\mleft[P\mright]=nTr[P]=n. This'll be an important fact later.

To check this, let's study this matrix elementwise. The matrix elements can be extracted by picking out columns of XXX; e.g., if we denote the iii-th column of XXX as ψi\psi_iψi​, then the (i,j)\left(i, j\right)(i,j) element of PPP is

Nice, so EX[P]\mathbb{E}_X\mleft[P\mright]EX​[P] is diagonal, as we guessed. Let's solve for the diagonal elements PiiP_{ii}Pii​. First, it'll be useful to make note of the following facts:

In the first line, I separated the one correlated component from all the independent ones. In the second line, I interpreted K∖i−1/2ϕiK_{\setminus i}^{-1/2} \phi_iK∖i−1/2​ϕi​ as a sort of "ZCA-like" whitening, and wrote the equation in terms of the whitened vector ziz_izi​. Now let's take the expectation. The only random variable is ∥zi(∥2\norm{z_i}^2​zi​(​2. The second part is a leave-one-out approximation: since d≫nd \gg nd≫n, removing any one feature leaves KKK essentially unchanged, so for all intents and purposes K∖i≈K∖jK_{\setminus i} \approx K_{\setminus j}K∖i​≈K∖j​ for any iii and jjj. All together, we get EX[P]≈Λ(Λ+κI)−1\mathbb{E}_X\mleft[P\mright] \approx \Lambda \left(\Lambda + \kappa I\right)^{-1}EX​[P]≈Λ(Λ+κI)−1. Using the projector fact from earlier, we know it satisfies the equation Tr[Λ(Λ+κI)−1]=n\mathrm{Tr}\mleft[\Lambda \left(\Lambda + \kappa I\right)^{-1}\mright] = nTr[Λ(Λ+κI)−1]=n, so this constrains the value of κ\kappaκ.

Notice that this solution is exactly what ridge regression would give if it had access to the population covariance Λ\LambdaΛ and was regularized with ridge κ\kappaκ. For this reason, the constant κ\kappaκ is called the effective regularization: even though we didn't explicitly regularize, the finite dataset acts like a ridge regularizer. Directions with λi≫κ\lambda_i \gg \kappaλi​≫κ are learned, and directions with λi≪κ\lambda_i \ll \kappaλi​≪κ are not.

This is a deep idea: in least-squares linear regression, there is really only one kind of regularization; weight decay, finite dataset, and even early stopping all act in essentially the same way, and the behavior of the final estimator depends mainly on whichever of those constraints is the strongest.
